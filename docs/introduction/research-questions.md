# Research questions and historical sources

Imagine a corpus of 1,000 manuscripts from 11th- to 13th-century Tibet.

A single manuscript, read on its own, can tell you its title, author, scribe, subject,
when and where it was written, and the people, places and works mentioned inside it.

Looking at the manuscript alone, though, you can't easily answer questions that only make
sense across the whole collection:

- Which places were most active, and when?
- Who collaborated with whom?
- Which works are copied, cited, or transmitted most often?
- How do networks of people, places, and texts change over time?

Answering those questions means turning each manuscript's contents into
structured data: the same kind of information, recorded
the same way, for every manuscript, so it can be compared and queried across the whole
corpus.
