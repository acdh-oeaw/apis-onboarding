# Historical sources

Prosopographical projects draw on whatever record of a person, place or event has
survived. The same project usually combines several kinds:

## Manuscripts

Handwritten documents: a copy of a text, a letter, a register. A manuscript carries its
own facts (who wrote it, when, where) as well as the people, places and works it
mentions or is *about*.

## Biographies

A connected account of one person's life, written by someone else or by the person
themselves. Rich in detail, but narrative: the facts are embedded in prose, not listed
out.

## Inscriptions

Short texts carved or written on a durable object: a monument, a tomb, a building. Often
fragmentary, and frequently the only surviving trace of a person or event.

## Accounting Books

Handwritten lists of types of employments, payments, and principals.

## Newspaper Articles

Information is not only in the article itself, but also in its metadata: who wrote
the article, when was it written, for which magazine? Are photographs attached, which
photographer took those, who is depicted?

## Other historical records

Administrative registers, parish records, correspondence, legal documents, and anything
else that records who did what, where, and when. Each has its own conventions, but all of
them face the same problem: a source on its own is hard to compare with another source.
